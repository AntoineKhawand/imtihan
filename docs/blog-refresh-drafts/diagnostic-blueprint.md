A diagnostic assessment blueprint lists the prerequisite skills a short test should reveal, the evidence each question collects and how teachers will interpret the answers. For parallel classes, agree on that blueprint before drafting the papers. The purpose is to plan teaching from comparable evidence, not to produce an early ranking of students.

An AI tool can help create questions from the agreed brief, but matching chapter names or total marks does not make two assessments equivalent. Teachers still need to compare the reasoning demanded by the questions and agree on what counts as evidence of understanding.

## Begin with the next teaching unit

Write down the skills students need for the unit you are about to teach. Separate essential prerequisites from material you plan to introduce later. A diagnostic paper should not penalize students for content they have not been taught.

For example, a mathematics teacher preparing a functions unit might check algebraic manipulation, graph reading and explaining a conclusion. A physics teacher starting a mechanics unit might check unit conversion, reading a data table and relating a graph to a physical statement. Use the objectives relevant to your actual level and course rather than adopting these examples unchanged.

## Create a short evidence map

For each objective, choose a task and note what a successful response would show. An illustrative twelve-mark classroom diagnostic could contain three tasks of four marks each. One might check a prerequisite calculation, one might ask students to interpret unfamiliar data, and one might require a short explanation.

The number of marks is not a claim about an official paper structure. It is a planning device. If the explanation is the most important prerequisite, give it enough space and credit rather than allowing arithmetic to dominate the score.

A teacher note for each task should answer three questions: which skill is being assessed, what common misconception the response may reveal, and what teaching action would follow from that evidence. This prevents the diagnostic test from becoming just another score in a spreadsheet.

## Agree on marking before generating alternatives

Ask the teachers of parallel classes to review one worked answer and discuss borderline responses. Identify which mistakes indicate a missing concept and which are minor slips. Do not assume that every wrong numeric answer has the same teaching implication.

If classes use different paper versions, compare the number of reasoning steps, the amount of scaffolding and the difficulty of the data interpretation. Familiar contexts, hints and intermediate values can make one version easier even when its total marks match another.

Use a [shared exam-difficulty review](/blog/exam-standardization) to moderate the drafts. A shared brief is a useful starting point; it does not automatically standardize the resulting papers or teachers' judgments.

## Give the generator a specific brief

Here is a reusable teacher prompt:

> Draft a short diagnostic assessment using only the prerequisite skills listed in my selected chapters and description. Include one calculation, one interpretation task and one explanation. For each question, provide a worked answer, clear mark criteria and a note on the misconception an incorrect response could reveal. Do not introduce the new unit's untaught methods.

In Imtihan, select the correct curriculum, level and chapters before generation. If you upload a previous diagnostic, distinguish between using its content, taking inspiration from the exercise style and copying its section arrangement. Review any extracted structure before generating the paper.

## Turn the results into a teaching plan

Review patterns by objective rather than looking only at the average score. If students calculate successfully but cannot explain a graph, the next lesson should address interpretation. If a question confused otherwise capable students because of its wording, revise the question instead of treating every response as a learning deficit.

Keep the follow-up manageable: identify the prerequisite that needs a short recap, the misconception that needs a worked example and the skill that students can practise independently. After teaching, use a different question to check whether the explanation transfers to a new context.

## Final checks before classroom use

Confirm that the paper is short enough for its diagnostic purpose, that the wording is accessible and that teachers agree on the marking criteria. Solve the questions, inspect any diagrams and open the final export. Keep the teacher notes and worked solutions separate from the student paper.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Should a diagnostic assessment copy the official exam format?**
Only if that format serves the diagnostic purpose. A short prerequisite check often needs a simpler composition than a full practice examination.

**Q: Can parallel classes take different diagnostic questions?**
Yes, but teachers should compare the skills, scaffolding and reasoning demanded by each version. Equal duration and marks alone do not demonstrate comparability.

**Q: Is the total score enough to plan the next lesson?**
No. Review the evidence for each objective and distinguish conceptual gaps from arithmetic slips, unclear wording or unfamiliar representations.
